02

Don't Go Intellectual

The regression Jade opened the lecture with.

From the lecture

Jade: "If you find the emotional charge, that's everything that you've been looking for. And if you start going intellectual, you completely bypassed it."

The tell

Client gets emotional → coach asks a deeper question about it ("Jade, tell me more about that"). It's the natural instinct from therapy and normal coaching, and it's the wrong move here. Therapy is valuable, but it's not built to get into the body and the subconscious the way this work needs to. Once you spot a charge, the move is to work it somatically, not to talk about it.

03

Get Into The Work Fast

Skip the catch-up. Open with the real question.

From the lecture

Jade: "If there's something I could snap my fingers and make it disappear right now in this moment, what is going on for you? That's how you get into the work deeply."

Skip "how are you / how'd things go since last time." None of that is wrong, exactly — it's just intellectual processing again, and this work is only one-third Retrain (the lived repetition). The other two-thirds, Rewrite and Rewire, need the session time. With an established long-term client there's room for a brief connection point first; in a short Coaching Lab session, go straight in.

04

Two Tools: Breath & Beep

To drop someone into a subconscious state quickly.

Stress / brain breath

20 activating breaths (double inhale) → 10-second hold → 5 relaxing breaths. As homework, give it as a count: 20, 10, 5.

11-minute Beep session

Pull this in any time a client is being too intellectual/talky, or hasn't been doing their own between-session work (deeps, deltas, meditations). If they're already doing that work on their own, you usually don't need to force it into the session.

05

Catching The Emotion

The three-part move, and the somatic signal most coaches miss.

"Stay here. Feel this. What's coming up?"

Clients will try to move past a feeling the moment it surfaces. Don't let them wriggle away — the three-part line above holds them there. Closing the eyes when emotion surfaces helps too: it detaches them slightly and pushes them deeper instead of letting them talk their way out.

Watch for movement

Swaying, twitching, a neck snap, involuntary motion — this is a good sign, not something to settle. It means the client is deep in it. Invite it rather than interrupt it: "Does it want you to move? Let it move through you."

06

Breadcrumbs & Reconsolidation

Why the coach shouldn't be the one to supply the insight.

Drop breadcrumbs early. Plant an "always be learning" frame in normal conversation before the emotional work starts, so the client is primed to surface their own counterexample once the charge comes up later.

Reconsolidation vs. extinction

If the client surfaces the counterexample themselves, that's a true reconsolidation event — the new story now dominates. If they don't and the coach gives it to them instead, that's only an extinction/inhibition event: a competing story sitting alongside the old one, not a full rewrite. Most good coaching gets to extinction and stops there. Most coaching and therapy doesn't even get that far.

07

Live Demo: Working The Thread

Presenting issue: an unresponsive attorney. Root: a father who made her feel stupid.

From the demo

Volunteer: "In the last three weeks, he's disappeared on me… have I been stupid again?"

Jade: "Tell me about 'disappearing.'"

Jade: "Where is this familiar? Is there any other place in your life where this happens?"

Volunteer: "I could probably tie that back to my father."

The eight moves, narrated live

  1. Find the thread in her own words ("disappeared") and reflect it back open-ended rather than asking a leading question.
  2. Follow the thread, not every detail — her own next line ("been stupid again") is the real material.
  3. Battle-test the hypothesis instead of asserting it. "This could have something to do with your father, may not — I don't know. What's your intuition?" Never declare it outright; that's "therapising."
  4. Once she confirms it herself, go straight to the felt sense — location, temperature, texture ("hot," "in the gut").
  5. Separate the parts and locate them spatially. Her "little girl" lived in her gut and throat; her "next-level self" was located outside her body, over her shoulder. Real technique, not incidental detail.
  6. Stage the confrontation scene — the wounded child and the original figure in a remembered moment, then bring the adult self into the room.
  7. Bring it back to the present problem (the lawyer) and check whether the felt charge has actually shifted.
  8. Close with a specific, checkable commitment tied to the next-level self, not the wounded part: "Is this the little girl's job, or the next-level self's job?"
08

Debrief: What Actually Happened

Jade names his own miss, live. Then the group offers three other valid entry points.

Jade's own debrief

Jade: "I did not fully get a rewrite that I was trying to get… notice how she completely avoided the father, which to me is avoiding the lawyer."

Jade was steering toward the volunteer standing up to her father directly — that would have signalled a fuller rewrite. She avoided it, handing the moment to the "little girl" instead of keeping the adult self in charge. That avoidance is itself diagnostic: avoiding the father in the visualisation mirrors avoiding the lawyer in real life. Jade said plainly, on the call, that he probably only landed an extinction/inhibition event, and wasn't confident she'd actually follow through — the charge wasn't strong enough.

This is normal, not failure. Most sessions land here. The point of a Coaching Lab is watching exactly where it stalled, in real time, which sets up what to try differently next session.

Three other valid entry points (the group debrief)

  • Annalise: would have made the internal somatic connection between the two body locations directly ("what's in the gut that needs to come out through the throat?") instead of jumping back to the intellectual "tell me about this lawyer now."
  • Lisa: noticed a parallel money/authority story running alongside the father story ("I'm paying $750/hour so they must be smarter than me") — an equally valid thread to have pulled instead.
  • Heather: flagged that the lawyer came back into the conversation too fast after the somatic work with the father — staying in the somatic longer before returning to the presenting problem likely would have worked better.

Many paths lead to the same root. Don't assume your first read is the only right one, and don't feel obligated to circle back to the presenting problem once root work is underway — it isn't always necessary.

09

When A Client Is Stuck

Heather's three difficult cases: emotional in-session, but never connecting the dot after.

Reframe failure first

Regulation/inhibition work is valuable even without a full rewrite. ~3 months in is normal before a coach has a real handle on a client, and even then you're still peeling layers, not "done." Getting assigned the hardest cases is a sign of capability, not a problem — the actual block to fix is the coach's own perfectionism about every session landing cleanly.

The direct move for a stuck client

Jade: "We're getting nice emotional movement, but we're not seeing the story yet, and I think it's because you're not doing the classes between sessions. So we're going to start our next few sessions with Beep."

For a client who can't visualise or feel anything at all ("fantasia" block, high armour)

Run the Beep session, then have them journal by hand for ~10 minutes (half the session), then discuss the journal together. This puts the surfacing work on the client instead of the coach doing all of it.

Journaling modality hierarchy

Handwriting (best, fullest brain processing) > eyes-closed dictation > typing (worst — eyes-open, hands-on-keyboard keeps the brain partly checked out).

10

Coach Self-Regulation

Prompted by a coach naming that certain material makes her cry in-session.

"You cannot allow that to happen… part of the therapeutic bond happens from the intention that this person is here to hold this space."

The hard rule: don't cry with a client

Crying pulls the coach out of the therapeutic role. Regulation and appropriateness are the first two lessons in doing emotional work at all. If you know beforehand you're not regulated enough, don't see clients that day — not a judgment call to push through, a hard stop.

Pre-session anchoring

Fast personal breath work (20/10/5) before going in if not in a good headspace, plus a stated mental frame: "I'm just a vehicle. I will step aside and let whatever needs to come through, come through. This is not me doing this."

Post-session discharge

Physically shed the energy afterward rather than carrying it — Jade's own example was an improvised HAKA-style release (chest, growling, movement) after an especially heavy session. Cited research: exercise helps prevent difficult emotional material from "sticking" the way sitting still does. At minimum, get a workout in after a heavy session.

If emotion does surface in-session

Name and join without collapsing into it: "This is heavy. Let's feel this together." Never "gushing" — that's the least therapeutic thing you can do. If literal tears surface, name them without owning them: "I'm feeling tears right now. Not sure whose they are."

The self-disclosure test

Before sharing anything personal with a client, ask: "Am I sharing this for me, or for the client's benefit?" Only share if it's for their benefit, keep it contained, and return immediately to their material.

Cautionary evidence, from the client's side: a client's prior therapist cried with her, and she experienced it as unhelpful — it put the burden on her to manage the therapist's emotions and made her feel she couldn't be fully vulnerable. Direct evidence for the rule above, from the receiving end.

The "do-it-for-me" client pattern

Some clients unconsciously want the coach to do the emotional/insight work for them. Beep + independent journaling (not coach-led synthesis) is the structural fix — it hands the reps back to the client instead of the coach absorbing them.

Who decides if a session worked? The client does — not the coach's in-the-room read. A session that felt incomplete to the coach can still have cracked something open that keeps working afterward. Whoever's in the room, saying what they're saying, is enough for the work that's supposed to happen there — even an apparent miss can still be doing its job.
